Qualified Overtime Estimator for California Firefighters

This route assumes nonexempt fire protection activities and a valid employer-adopted FLSA 7(k) work period of 7 through 28 days. It does not model ordinary 40-hour overtime, exempt employees, or comp-time cash payouts. Confirm the applicable route with payroll.

The federal overtime deduction does not apply to every hour your paystub calls overtime. It reaches only the premium half of overtime the FLSA itself requires, and fire protection runs under the special 7(k) rules that put that line much higher than 40 hours. This tool estimates what actually qualifies for you. Education only.

How to use it

Seven fields, and two you may need payroll for

Choose your filing status and enter your estimated 2026 modified adjusted gross income, which drives the phaseout. Pick the 7(k) work period formally adopted by your department, enter the hours you work in a typical one, the FLSA regular rate payroll applies to those periods, and your federal marginal bracket. The last field is your estimated annual hours above the applicable federal ceiling that required an overtime premium.

The regular rate and the hours above the ceiling are payroll questions. The regular rate is not always base hourly pay because federal rules can include other remuneration. Ask payroll for the count of hours that required an FLSA overtime premium. Count a qualifying hour even if it was paid at double time; the estimator applies only the potentially qualified minimum half-rate premium. If you never cross the ceiling, enter zero.

You get back an estimated qualified overtime amount, the estimated deduction after the cap and the phaseout are applied, and a rough federal benefit at the bracket you selected. If the hours you typed in a typical work period do not square with the annual figure you entered, the tool prints a cross-check note so you can see the mismatch.

This calculator computes locally and does not itself submit or store your inputs. The surrounding site may have separate analytics or network activity.

The rules

What the estimator is applying

Every rule below comes from the statute or from published federal guidance. None of it is a house interpretation of how the deduction ought to work.

The FLSA 7(k) ceiling

Fire protection employees fall under a special overtime provision rather than the 40 hour week. A department may establish a work period from 7 through 28 consecutive days. The federal table assigns each length its own ceiling, including 212 hours in 28 days, 159 hours in 21 days, 106 hours in 14 days, and 53 hours in 7 days. Only hours above the ceiling that applies to your department are federally required overtime. Hours your memorandum of understanding pays as overtime below that line are wages, and they do not count for this deduction.

Only the premium half counts

On qualifying hours paid at time and a half, the deduction reaches the extra half, not the whole payment. The tool multiplies your qualifying hours by the FLSA regular rate you enter by 0.5 for exactly that reason.

If a qualifying FLSA overtime hour is paid at double time, the minimum half-rate premium can still qualify. The extra premium above the FLSA minimum does not. Holiday or weekend premium pay for hours that are not FLSA overtime is outside the qualified amount.

The caps

$12,500 per return for a single filer or head of household, and $25,000 for married filing jointly. Anything above that is trimmed to the cap before the phaseout is applied.

The phaseout

The deduction is reduced by $100 for each whole $1,000 of modified adjusted gross income above $150,000 single or $300,000 joint. A partial $1,000 is disregarded. The tool rounds down accordingly.

Married filing separately

Not eligible. If you select it, the tool says so and stops rather than printing a number that does not exist.

The number that actually controls your return

Your employer reports qualified overtime on your W-2 in Box 12 with code TT. Use the reported figure when accurate, request employer corrections for inaccurate reporting, and follow current IRS instructions. If what your employer reports and what you expected are far apart, that is a question for your payroll office and your CPA, and it is worth asking before you file rather than after.

One California detail that catches people: the state does not conform to this deduction. Whatever it does for your federal return, your California return sees no benefit from it.

Straight talk

What this estimator does not tell you

This is an education tool that exists mostly to show people a smaller number than the headline led them to expect. Its limits matter.

  • It is not a tax return, and it is not tax advice. Confirm your situation with a licensed CPA before you plan around any figure here. The deduction interacts with the rest of your return in ways a single-purpose calculator cannot see.
  • It cannot count your hours for you. The hours above the ceiling are an input you supply. If that number is a guess, the output is a guess. Payroll holds the real answer.
  • Check the reported amount. Use accurate employer-reported 2026 Box 12 code TT amounts and current IRS instructions. Request corrected reporting if the reported amount is wrong; this estimate cannot resolve a reporting error.
  • It excludes premium pay beyond the FLSA minimum. Double time on a qualifying FLSA overtime hour can still contain a qualified half-rate component. Only the extra premium beyond that minimum is excluded. Holiday or weekend premiums for hours that are not FLSA overtime are excluded. Comp time used as paid leave generally is not qualified overtime compensation. Cash paid for accrued unused comp time can qualify to the extent it represents the required FLSA premium; this route does not calculate comp-time payouts.
  • There is no FICA effect and no California benefit. This is a federal income tax deduction only. Social Security and Medicare withholding do not change, and your state return does not.
  • The federal benefit line is rough. It multiplies the deduction by the marginal bracket you picked. Real tax outcomes depend on your full return, credits, and whether the deduction moves you across a bracket line.
  • Withholding does not adjust on its own. The 2026 Form W-4 lets you account for an expected deduction in Step 4(b). If you leave it alone, any benefit arrives when you file rather than in your checks.
  • The deduction is temporary. Current law covers tax years 2025 through 2028. This calculator uses 2026 caps, thresholds, and reporting, which can change.

The people with the largest overtime lines are frequently the people the phaseout targets hardest, so a big paystub number and a big deduction are not the same thing.

A useful way to run it

Before you run it, ask payroll how many hours last year were above the applicable FLSA 7(k) ceiling and required an overtime premium. A paystub label alone is not enough, and total contract or MOU overtime may include hours below the federal ceiling. Use payroll's qualified-hour count, then take the estimate to a CPA.
